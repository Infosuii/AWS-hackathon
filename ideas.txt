1. Blindside Threat Velocity (BTV)
The Concept
An interactive Quarterback vision engine that tracks where the QB is looking in real time and calculates how fast unseen pass rushers are charging at them from outside their field of view.
How It Works
Vision Field Modeling: Reads the QB’s live body orientation angle (o) on every frame and projects a 120-degree forward vision wedge representing their active field of view.
Blindspot Detection: Flags any pass rusher located outside this 120-degree wedge as a "blindside threat."
Closure Velocity: Measures how fast that blindside defender is closing the distance to the QB (in yards per second) while remaining undetected.
Threat Alerting: Triggers a high-risk pressure event when a blindside rusher gets within 10 yards of the QB at high speed.
How Easy It Is to Visualise as a Final Product
Effort Level: Very Easy
Plotly Implementation: You render standard 2D scatter dots for players, then add a single filled polar polygon (a semi-transparent triangle wedge) extending forward from the QB's (x,y) coordinates.
Visual Impact: High-contrast and immediate—when a blindside rusher approaches, you simply draw a bold red line between the rusher dot and QB dot. Judges instantly understand the metric without reading a manual.
2. Structural Breach Index (SBI)
The Concept
An offensive line protection dashboard that builds a geometric perimeter around pass blockers and pinpoints the exact frame, angle, and defender that causes the pocket wall to break.
How It Works
Protection Boundary: Connects the positions of all five offensive linemen on every frame using scipy.spatial.ConvexHull to form a dynamic perimeter around the QB.
Breach Event Detection: Detects the exact frame when a pass rusher breaks inside that boundary polygon.
Weakest Link Tracking: Identifies which specific offensive lineman allowed the penetration (e.g., Left Tackle vs. Right Guard) and measures defender penetration speed upon crossing the boundary.
Structural Collapse Rate: Calculates the overall rate at which the protection perimeter shrinks from snap to throw or sack.
How Easy It Is to Visualise as a Final Product
Effort Level: Extremely Easy
Plotly Implementation: Scipy's ConvexHull directly returns the ordered boundary vertices for your 5 linemen. You pass those coordinates straight into a Plotly go.Scatter(fill='toself') trace.
Visual Impact: Highly intuitive—the pocket polygon displays in Green at snap, turns Yellow as space shrinks, and turns Red with a target marker at the exact frame a rusher steps inside.





The Decoy Value Index (DVI)

How valuable is a receiver who never gets the ball?

The big idea

Consider two wide receivers.

Receiver A

* Catches six passes.
* Gains 80 yards.

Receiver B

* Catches one pass.
* Gains 12 yards.

Based on conventional receiving statistics, Receiver A appears more productive.

But what if Receiver B repeatedly occupies a defender, opens a passing lane for a teammate and helps create space elsewhere?

Receiver B could be contributing to the offence in ways that receiving statistics don’t capture.

Your proposed metric would investigate the spatial value of a receiver’s movement when they aren’t the intended target.

How would you calculate it?

The tracking data contains player coordinates, while the scouting data identifies players whose role is Pass route and identifies the passer.

That gives you a starting point for identifying offensive routes and their relationship to the rest of the play.  

Step 1: Identify the receiver’s route.

Track the receiver’s position from the snap until pass release.

Step 2: Measure defensive displacement.

Track the nearest defender and measure how their position changes during the route.

For example, calculate how far a defender moves while tracking a particular receiver.

Step 3: Measure space created for teammates.

For another offensive receiver, calculate their separation from the nearest defender over the same period.

If the first receiver’s route coincides with the second receiver gaining space, that is a potentially interesting pattern.

Step 4: Calculate a preliminary score.

One possible definition is:

DVI_i =
\frac{\text{Teammate Separation Gains During Route }i}
{\text{Number of Eligible Plays}}

You could improve this by measuring the change in separation relative to the start of the play.

A receiver who repeatedly runs routes during which teammates gain space would receive a higher score.

What makes this different?

Most basic receiving analysis focuses on the receiver who catches the ball.

Your metric investigates the potential contribution of players who don’t receive it.

This is particularly interesting for:

* Receivers who draw coverage away from teammates.
* Tight ends who run routes that occupy underneath defenders.
* Players who help open space for another receiver.

The visualisation

Create a route-level comparison.

Show:

1. The decoy receiver’s route.
2. The defender’s movement.
3. The teammate’s changing separation.
4. The play’s final outcome.

Highlight plays where a receiver doesn’t get targeted but a teammate gains separation.

The critical limitation

A receiver’s route and a teammate’s separation gain occurring together doesn’t prove that the route caused the gain.

You would need to account for route combinations, defensive schemes and the intended design of the play to make stronger causal claims.

Difficulty: 4/5
Originality potential: Very high
Three-hour feasibility: Moderate

 Defensive Domino Effect (DDE)

The question: Which offensive movements trigger a chain reaction across the defence?

Picture this sequence:

1. Receiver A runs across the formation.
2. Defender A follows.
3. Defender B shifts to compensate.
4. A third defender moves to cover the space Defender B vacated.
5. Another receiver gains an opportunity.

The interesting part isn’t simply how far one defender moves. It’s how much defensive movement one offensive player’s route appears to trigger across the entire unit.

Possible metric

\text{DDE}_i =
\frac{\text{Total defensive displacement associated with route }i}
{\text{Route distance}_i}

You could also measure how many defenders change direction or alter their distance from offensive players during the route.

The output: A leaderboard of receivers who generate the largest defensive chain reactions.

The limitation: Tracking data alone cannot prove that one route caused every subsequent movement. The score measures an associated reaction, not confirmed causation.
